Step 1 · Orientation

Two gases. One temperature. Same molecular speed?

Scenario T5: compare helium and argon at 300 K with equal particle numbers and equal volumes. Work through the steps in order — learn, predict, then unlock the simulation.

The system

Two sealed rigid containers. One holds helium, the other argon. Both have the same volume and the same number of atoms. Each gas sample is the system; the containers and reservoir are the surroundings.

Energy transfer

Both containers touch one reservoir at temperature T. Heat flows until both reach that temperature, then stops. The walls are rigid, so W = 0 for both samples.

The common error

Most students answer "same temperature, so same speed." That is wrong, and the reason why is the whole point of this project.

  • The two systems differ in exactly one way: the mass of one atom. Volume, particle number and temperature are all matched.
  • Because only mass differs, any difference you observe must be a mass effect. That is what makes this a clean comparison.
Step 2 · Fundamentals

Four ideas you need before predicting

1
Temperature measures energy, not speed

For an ideal gas, temperature is defined through the average translational kinetic energy of one molecule:

Definition of temperature⟨KE⟩ = 3⁄2 kB T

Notice what is missing: no mass, no molar mass, no gas identity. Two gases at the same T must have the same ⟨KE⟩ per molecule.

▸ Why is mass absent? Click to reveal

This is the equipartition theorem. At equilibrium every independent way of storing energy holds an average of 1⁄2kBT. A molecule can move along x, y and z, giving three ways, so it averages 3 × 1⁄2kBT = 3⁄2kBT. The count depends on how many directions it can move in, never on how heavy it is (Ling et al., 2016).

⟨KE⟩ HELIUM6.21× 10⁻²¹ J
⟨KE⟩ ARGON6.21× 10⁻²¹ J
However far you drag it, the two numbers stay identical. That is what "same temperature" means.

2
What "root-mean-square" actually means

The symbol vrms appears everywhere in this topic, so it is worth building it from scratch. Read the name backwards — it tells you the three operations in order:

Read right to left SQUARE every molecule's speed  →  take the MEAN of those squares  →  take the ROOT of that mean
In symbols vrms = √⟨v²⟩ = √( (v1² + v2² + … + vN²) / N )

Worked example with just three molecules

Suppose three molecules move at 300, 500 and 700 m/s.

OperationWorkingResult
Ordinary mean speed(300 + 500 + 700) ÷ 3500 m/s
① Square each speed90 000 · 250 000 · 490 000m²/s²
② Mean of the squares830 000 ÷ 3276 667 m²/s²
③ Root of that mean√276 667526 m/s

The rms speed (526 m/s) is larger than the ordinary mean (500 m/s), even though both describe the same three molecules.

▸ Why use rms instead of the ordinary average? Click to reveal

Because energy depends on v², not on v. Kinetic energy is 1⁄2mv², so to get the average energy you must average v² first, then convert back to a speed. That is precisely the "square, mean, root" recipe.

Using the ordinary mean would give the wrong energy. In the example above, 1⁄2m(500)² = 125 000m is not the true average energy; the correct value is 1⁄2m(276 667) = 138 333m, which matches 1⁄2m(526)².

Squaring also gives extra weight to the fast molecules in the upper tail, which is why vrms always exceeds the plain mean speed. The two are never equal unless every molecule has exactly the same speed.

Summary: vrms is the one speed that, if every molecule had it, would give the correct total kinetic energy. That is why it — and not the ordinary average — appears in the temperature equation.

3
From energy to speed — where the mass enters

Now combine Lessons 1 and 2. Set the average kinetic energy equal to the temperature definition and solve for speed.

▸ Line 1 — start here
1⁄2 m ⟨v²⟩ = 3⁄2 kB T

The left side is the average kinetic energy written with the mean square speed from Lesson 2.

▸ Line 2 — isolate the mean square speed
⟨v²⟩ = 3 kB T / m

The mass has landed in the denominator. Equal T no longer means equal ⟨v²⟩ unless the masses also match.

▸ Line 3 — take the root, then switch to molar units
vrms = √(3 kB T / m) = √(3 R T / M)

These are the same equation. Multiply inside the root by NA/NA: since kBNA = R and m·NA = M, the swap is exact. Use m in kg per atom with kB, or M in kg per mole with R. Never mix them, and never leave M in g/mol.

▸ Line 4 — the ratio for two gases at one temperature
vHe / vAr = √(MAr / MHe)

Temperature cancels completely. The speed ratio depends only on the two masses and is identical at every temperature. This one line answers the assigned question: heating both gases can never close the gap.

4
A gas has a spread of speeds, not one speed

At any instant molecules range from nearly stationary to several times the average. The Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution gives the probability of each speed, and vrms is one summary number taken from it.

Maxwell–Boltzmann speed distribution f(v) = 4π ( m / 2π kB T )3/2 · v² · e−mv² / 2kBT

Three summary speeds are commonly quoted and they are not equal:

vp = √(2RT/M)  ·  v̄ = √(8RT/πM)  ·  vrms = √(3RT/M)
Their ratio is always 1 : 1.13 : 1.22 — for every gas, at every temperature.
▸ Why is vrms the biggest of the three? Click to reveal

Straight from Lesson 2: squaring before averaging gives extra weight to the fast molecules in the long upper tail. So the root-mean-square always exceeds the plain mean v̄, which in turn exceeds the peak vp. The ordering is fixed by the shape of the curve, which is why the ratio is universal.

Both curves enclose the same total area — every atom has some speed. Argon is tall and narrow; helium is broad and low with a long fast tail.

What these lessons established

  • Temperature sets energy. ⟨KE⟩ = 3⁄2kBT has no mass in it, so equal temperature means equal energy per atom.
  • vrms means square, then mean, then root. It is used because energy depends on v², and it is always larger than the plain average speed.
  • Mass enters only when you solve for speed. vrms = √(3RT/M) puts M in the denominator, so heavier means slower.
  • The speed ratio is √(MAr/MHe) and temperature cancels out of it — the gap can never be closed by heating.

Limitations

  • No condensation. Argon liquefies at 87 K and helium at 4.2 K; the model keeps reporting gas speeds below those points, so a red warning appears.
  • Atoms pass through each other. The distribution is correct but individual trajectories are not.
  • 60 dots in two dimensions at reduced speed. A real sample is 10²³ atoms in 3-D at hundreds of m/s.
  • Monatomic only. Setting the mass near 28 g/mol does not make the model nitrogen.

What to take from this step

  • Every green number is identical for the two gases; every orange number differs by the factor √(MB/MA).
  • The pressures match because helium's higher collision rate is exactly cancelled by argon's larger momentum per collision — the net effect readout stays at 1.00×.
Step 5 · Guided scenarios

Four cases, each predicted before it is loaded

Decide your answer first, then load the setup and check it.

SCENARIO 1The assigned case — helium versus argon at 300 K

Predict: which readouts match and which differ, and by what factor.

Expect: green readouts identical; speed ratio 3.16.

Why: temperature fixes 1⁄2m⟨v²⟩, so it equalises the product of mass and mean square speed, not the speed itself. Argon is about 10× heavier, so its mean square speed is about 10× smaller and its rms speed √10 ≈ 3.16× smaller.

SCENARIO 2Heat both to 900 K — does the gap close?

Predict: by what factor does each speed change, and what happens to the ratio?

Expect: both speeds × √3 = 1.73; the ratio does not move from 3.16.

Why: the ratio is √(MAr/MHe) and temperature cancels out of it. No temperature exists at which these two gases move alike. This is the most direct answer to the assigned question.

SCENARIO 3 · CONTROLMake both boxes helium

Predict: what must the speed ratio become, and what should the curves do?

Expect: ratio exactly 1.00; the two curves overlap and the distributions superimpose.

Why: this is the control test. It proves mass is the only difference built into the model — if anything else still differed, this setup would expose it.

Step 8 · Reflect and cite

What this teaches, what it leaves out, and where it came from

What it teaches well

  • It separates energy from speed. Watching ⟨KE⟩ stay locked while the speeds sit a factor of 3.16 apart makes the definition of temperature concrete.
  • The gap never closes. Dragging from 20 K to 1500 K and seeing the ratio frozen is stronger than algebra, because you try to disprove it and fail.
  • Equal pressure from opposite causes. More collisions but weaker, versus fewer but stronger, turn out to cancel exactly.

What it leaves out

  • Atom-to-atom collisions, mean free path and diffusion.
  • Real-gas behaviour and condensation.
  • Polyatomic gases, where rotation stores energy that is not translational.
  • Mixtures — the gases stay in separate containers, so partial pressures are untouched.

One improvement

Add a mixed container mode where both gases share one volume. Students would see equal ⟨KE⟩ and unequal speeds while the two gases contribute partial pressures that add — connecting this directly to Dalton's law.
